Michael Wade says 2026 is the era of the COO. I'm betting on a slower, harder version of that story.

July 27, 2026 · 4 min · Operations leadership

Michael Wade, a professor at IMD, has a prediction for 2026: the COO becomes the most influential AI leader in the C-suite, ahead of the CIO, the CTO, and the CMO. I hope he's right. I don't think he will be, not this year, and not without a lot more work than most companies are currently planning to do.

Here's Wade's actual case. Writing for IMD's 2026 AI trends piece in December, Wade argued that the biggest AI returns this year won't come from customer-facing tools. They'll come from deep operations: efficiency, error elimination, safety, cost, productivity. "The highest ROI will come from deep operations, not customer-facing AI," he wrote. And because that's operational territory, he expects the COO to become "its most influential champion within the C-suite in 2026, overtaking the CIO, CTO, and CMO in many companies."

I agree with the opportunity. COOs should be stepping into this. I've spent enough time in operations transformation to know the upside Wade's describing is real, and the logic that gets you there is sound. What I'm a lot less bullish on is the timeline.

In my experience actually diagnosing how ready organizations are for this kind of shift, operations carries some of the heaviest organizational headwinds in the building. Big teams. High headcounts. Manual process that's already survived multiple previous waves of "this changes everything" technology, because nobody ever funded the work to enable teams to work in new ways, just the cost to buy the tech.

And there's real data pointing the same direction. Grant Thornton's 2026 AI Impact Survey found that only 7% of COOs say their own workforce is fully ready to adopt AI, versus 39% of CIOs and CTOs. COOs are also nearly three times as likely as CIOs and CTOs to say they're worried about the compliance exposure they're inheriting from agentic AI: 54% versus 20%. That's not a function gearing up to lead a championing moment. That's a function bracing for one.

This is where I'd put my clinician hat on, since diagnosing whether an organization is actually ready for this kind of change is a real part of what I do. Investment in operations is necessary. And potentially carries tempting returns. I'm not arguing against it. But it also carries a high risk of delayed returns if it isn't matched by aggressive investment in organizational design and change management, and most of the plans I'm seeing right now don't pair the two. New technologies land on teams that have real mindset shifts to make and habits to unlearn if they are to truly take advantage of them, and I've watched that exact thing unfold before. Technology ships on schedule into an organization unready to use it. Promised returns are unrealized. And there is general disappointment all around.

Here's my bet, and I'm putting a date on it so you can hold me to it. When the actual 2027 numbers come in for what happened across 2026, I expect an elite group of COOs to have genuinely championed this investment and pulled ahead. I also expect many, maybe most, to still be lagging, carrying real scar tissue from technology waves that promised transformation and delivered a lot of unused tooling instead.

Converting that majority won't happen by dropping new technology on operations teams and expecting them to figure out the rest on their own. It takes redesigned team structures, redefined roles, clear decision rights, and real support for the people living through the change. All of that costs money and takes time. Skipping it doesn't make the cost disappear. It just defers it, with interest. Companies that skip it will keep meeting COO resistance, active or passive, and see only muted returns on what they've invested in operations.

What would change my mind: if the 2027 numbers show COOs championing this broadly, roughly in line with Wade's prediction, without that parallel investment in people and structure, then I'm wrong about the mechanism. The prominence would be converting into leadership on the technology investment alone, and the change-management story I'm telling here wouldn't explain the gap.

To be clear, this isn't a prediction that operations leadership is set up to fail. Some COOs will close this gap this year, on Wade's timeline, and will deserve full credit when they do. The scar tissue I'm describing isn't a character flaw. It's a rational response to living through a few too many "this changes everything" rollouts that landed with a thud in the absence of organizational support for teams to absorb the technology well.

Let's see where the numbers land next year. I'd genuinely like to be wrong here and see results closer to what Wade's predicting. But whichever way the cohort splits, if you're facing apathy or resistance in operations to the promises of AI in 2027, look at your investment in organizational change management first. It's the most effective lever you've got for changing hearts and minds.

Where does your own operations team land on this? Set up to champion this work, or just handed the exposure?
