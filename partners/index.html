The shape of a joint pursuit

What I bring to your pursuit is the operating-model layer.

The diagnosis of whether the client’s structures, decision rights, and ways of working can absorb what you’re proposing to build, and the redesign work when they can’t. What you bring to mine is delivery capacity I don’t carry: I’m one accountable executive, deliberately, and when an engagement’s scope calls for a team, I reach for partners rather than pretending to be a bench.

You prime, I sub

Your client, your paper, your delivery. I take the operating-model workstream: the team structures, the capacity model, the decision rights that determine whether your build lands or gathers dust. The uncomfortable changes stay with the client to make; my job is making sure they’re named plainly and sequenced honestly.

I prime, you sub

My client, my paper. The engagement surfaces build work, and rather than referring it into the void, I contract your practice for the delivery while I hold the advisory layer and the client relationship.

Fit

What I look for in a partner.

Not a long list. A practice that’s senior and small enough that the people who sold the work do the work. Delivery quality you’d stake your own name on, because in a joint pursuit I am staking mine. And comfort with the division of discomfort: I name what has to change in the client’s organization, you don’t get dragged into that fight, and neither of us pretends the other’s lane is easy.

How the money works, conceptually

Every joint deal answers three questions: who found it, who manages the client through it, and who delivers it. The split follows those answers, agreed before pursuit, recorded in a shared register so nobody is reconstructing intentions six months later from memory and Slack scroll.

The actual numbers and the register rules are one login away.
