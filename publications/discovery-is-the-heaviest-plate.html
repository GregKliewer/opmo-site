Discovery is the heaviest plate on the barbell, dumbbell!

August 31, 2026 · 7 min · Product discovery

Many people I talk to today are shipping software faster than they were two years ago. And almost every one of them is treating the speed itself as the advantage. That’s the assumption I want to challenge this week.

I say that as someone who spent most of his career in the middle of the lifecycle. Platform, architecture, delivery. If you’d asked me five years ago where the game was won, I’d have pointed at the build, and I fixated on build speed the same way I’m about to caution you against. So this isn’t a lecture from “the discovery people”. I’m a late convert. But anyone who knows me will tell you, once I have my “road to Damascus” moment and accept a gospel, I can become the most obnoxious evangelist you’ll ever meet. I’m talking big tent revival kind of stuff. So buckle up for my a sermon on the subject of how neglecting discovery, or expecting tools to do it for you, is the paved road that will take you comfortably and quickly to a lake of burning disappointment.

The shape I keep drawing on the big tent’s whiteboard

Three stages every feature moves through: discovery, build, approval. The middle got dramatically faster. Agentic coding, generated everything. But the ends didn’t move. Discovery by and large still runs on the same channels, the same interviews, the same synthesis it ran on three years ago. Approvals still run, for the most part, through the same reviews and the same people.

Two things follow, mechanically. Upstream, a vacuum: the engine pulls ideas faster than discovery can validate them, so unvalidated ideas get built. Beautifully, quickly, and highly, highly speculatively. Downstream, a pile-up: output queues faster than review clears it.

Draw the effort that actually differentiates you now and it sits at the two ends, with a commodity in the middle. Like a dumbbell.

And the ends aren’t empty by accident. Budgets went to the engine because the engine is what vendors sell and what boards can see. Nobody stood up in a planning meeting and chose to starve discovery. But nobody staffed it either, and the people running it are still expected to feed a machine that got dramatically hungrier. That’s what I want to preach about today.

Last week I learned I’m not the only one drawing this analogy

On July 18 I registered the synopsis for a September conference talk in Toronto, built on exactly this shape, under the working title “What’s the opportunity, dumbbell?” (Yes, the pun is intentional, and I am the dumbbell in this scenario, let me be clear.) Then, only ten days later, Rich Mironov published a piece called Barbell-Shaped Product Roles. Same picture. The middle commoditizing, the weight moving to the ends, the product job getting heavier at both tips. Amazing!

Reminds me of the old story about Newton and Leibniz working out calculus at the same time, independently and without contact or knowledge of each other’s efforts. People have been debating who really did it first for centuries since. As a philosophy major, I always rooted for Leibniz. But that’s a silly argument. And let me tell you, Rich may be a Newton of product, but I am definitely no Leibniz in philosophy, product or anything else. Nor is an analogy anything close to calculus. So probably there will be less discussion about this coincidence in future generations. Or this one, for that matter. But, nevertheless, I am thoroughly enjoying the moment :)

And to be clear, our respective analogies are not precisely the same. Rich and I both name discovery as the weighty part on the left end of the equipment. But Rich loads the right end with go-to-market. I load it with approval: the governance, compliance and judgment work that decides whether the thing the engine built is safe to have. That difference matters, and it gets its own post next week. This week is about the left end.

The person who’s been on the left end all along

Teresa Torres has been making the left-end case since before the dumbbell had a name. In January she said on the Product Momentum podcast that AI is pulling product organizations back toward delivery, the exact imbalance her whole continuous discovery practice was built to correct, and that discovery gets more important as building gets cheaper, not less. She put the economics plainly in a session this spring: building is very cheap now, and quoting her, “that doesn’t mean we should build every idea we have.”

Building is very cheap now… “that doesn’t mean we should build every idea we have.”
Teresa Torres

Her sharpest warning is about what happens when teams point the engine at discovery itself. She has a name for the most common failure: lazy AI interview synthesis. Run the interview, dump the transcript into a chatbot, read the summary, move on. It feels like discovery. Her worry, and mine, is the scaled version of it: weak interview questions asked at scale, weak synthesis done at scale, and at the end of it the team builds what it had already decided to build.

In that case, the vacuum on the left end doesn’t stay empty. Worse, it fills with confident garbage.

The stance of hers I really like: AI in discovery should be additive, not a replacement. Faster real customer contact. Another perspective on synthesis you already did yourself. A coach that makes your next interview better than your last one. Not a synthetic customer standing in for a real one.

What the left end needs so that we are confident that the engine produces real value… not just glop

Staffing the left isn’t headcount first. It’s three mechanisms.

Formalize the judgement gate

The should-we-build-it gate becomes formal and non-skippable. When building cost something, that question asked itself. Now nothing asks it unless the operating model does.

Refine the practice of killing and erasing what doesn’t work

Kill criteria get written before the code. Every experiment ships with a measured outcome and a pre-agreed kill line, decided on day zero, so the day the reading comes in is a reading and not a debate. This is the piece I see missing almost everywhere, and it’s what converts discovery from a phase into an instrument.

Real people remain the focus, and tools get more, better data from them

Real customer contact accelerates, without substitution. AI-mediated interviews and always-on panels can push the rate of real contact well past what manual scheduling ever allowed. That’s the genuine acceleration available on the left end. Synthetic users aren’t part of it. Nobody credible treats a simulated customer as a source of truth about a real one. AI is good, but it’s not that good yet.

Where this doesn’t apply

If your build hasn’t actually sped up yet, the asymmetry this piece describes hasn’t arrived, and your bottleneck is still where it always was. Fix that first.

Build-to-learn prototyping is not the failure mode. Building a cheap prototype to test an assumption you named in advance is discovery, and the engine makes it wonderful. The failure mode is building the production version because nothing stopped you.

And the right end, the approval end, has its own version of this whole argument, especially in regulated shops. That’s next week.

One more thing before the questions

On September 9, Csaba Bereczki, Jamie Beach and I are putting a working version of both ends on stage at FOST Toronto, co-located with apidays. A fictional company, four interventions, and one paid feature that gets erased from existence on day 46 because the number written down on day zero said so. Come find us if you’re there.

In the meantime, if the engine has been running in your shop for a while, think back over what it produced. How many of the things you built at the new speed would you build again, knowing what you know now? And for the ones you wouldn’t, when did you actually know? My bet is that somebody in the room knew before the first line of code was written, and there was just no place in the machine to take advantage of that knowledge. That’s the question I’d spend some time contemplating: what would the front end of your lifecycle need to look like for that person to have been heard?
