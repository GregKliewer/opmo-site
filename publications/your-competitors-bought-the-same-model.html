Your competitors bought the same model you did

August 24, 2026 · 8 min · AI strategy

Somewhere in your organization right now, there is a deck comparing foundation models. Capability benchmarks, token pricing, vendor roadmaps, a scoring matrix with weighted criteria. Real work went into it. And in the organization you compete with most directly, there is a nearly identical deck, converging on a nearly identical shortlist, because everyone is choosing from the same three or four frontier models.

That is the problem with treating model selection as your AI strategy. Whatever you pick, your competitors can pick it too, next week, with a credit card. A decision that everyone can copy in a procurement cycle is not a strategy. It is a purchase.

What the model-selection question gets right

I want to be fair to the deck, because the instinct behind it is not wrong. Model choice carries real consequences, and in regulated environments some of them are hard constraints: where the data goes, what the vendor commits to contractually, what your regulator will accept, what a million tokens costs at your volume. Vendor diligence is genuine work and skipping it is how you end up explaining a data residency problem to an audit committee. None of that is wasted effort.

The problem is not that the model question gets asked. The problem is that in most organizations it is the only technical question that reaches the executive floor, and it is quietly assumed to be the one that decides the outcome. It is not. It is the smaller lever, and there is a decent illustration of just how much smaller.

The gap: the architecture bought more than the upgrade did

In March 2024, Andrew Ng presented an illustration assembled from results across multiple research teams, on HumanEval, a standard benchmark that measures whether an AI model can write correct code from a description. The numbers, from his telling: GPT-3.5 asked to answer in a single pass got 48.1%. GPT-4, same single-pass setup, got 67.0%. Then GPT-3.5 again, the cheaper and older model, wrapped in an agentic loop that let it plan, use tools, critique and revise its own output: up to 95.1%.

Read that as a budget decision. Upgrading the model bought about 19 points. Upgrading the architecture around the old model bought about 47, and finished roughly 28 points ahead of the better model running bare.

Two caveats, and they matter. This is an illustration stitched together from heterogeneous studies, not a controlled experiment. And it is two and a half years old now, which in this field is geological time; the specific models are museum pieces. So before leaning on it, I went looking for whether the shape of the result survived the model generations since. It did, and the current evidence is better than the illustration ever was.

The cleanest test came in July 2026, when a research team led by Oussama Ben Sghaier and Ahmed E. Hassan ran the experiment Ng’s illustration only gestured at, inverted: instead of fixing the wrapper and swapping models, they froze the model and varied only the harness, evaluating 35 sequential releases of one open-source coding agent against SWE-bench tasks. Quality moved release to release, and they traced the shifts to specific architectural changes in the wrapper, down to individual pull requests. Their motivating observation is the part I would frame and hang on the wall: practitioners routinely report quality regressions after a harness update and blame the model.

The modern rerun of the GPT-3.5 result exists too. Meta and Harvard’s Confucius Code Agent, published December 2025, is a scaffolding-first design, context management, persistent note-taking, modular tool use, and the paper reports 59% on SWE-bench Pro, exceeding commercial systems, in the authors’ words, “under identical repositories, model backends, and tool access.” Same model, same tools, better wrapper, better outcome.

And the frontier itself now makes the argument. By early 2026 the leading half-dozen models sat within about a point of each other on SWE-bench Verified, at least one of them an open-weight model anyone can download, while the same model run through different commercial harnesses spread by five to ten points on the harder SWE-bench Pro; one March 2026 analysis of the public leaderboards put harness-attributable swings at twenty-plus points against roughly one point for a frontier model swap. I hold those specific figures loosely, they are leaderboard arithmetic, not a controlled finding, but the direction is consistent across every source I checked, and it is the same direction Ng pointed in 2024. What would change my mind: a controlled result where swapping among frontier peers moves outcomes more than the harness design does. I have not found one.

This is not an AI-specific discovery, either. I have spent a couple of decades on delivery platforms in large regulated organizations, and I have watched the same scene play out with other capabilities. The vendor evaluation absorbs a quarter of executive attention. The capability arrives. And then the outcome gets decided by the part nobody put a deck together for: how the capability was wired into the way work actually flows. Two organizations buying the same product routinely get wildly different results from it, and the difference was never in the procurement matrix. The model-selection debate is the current instance of a very old pattern: the buy decision is visible and feels decisive, the wiring decision is invisible and actually is.

The four levers, in order

The architecture in Ng’s illustration is not exotic. He describes four design patterns, and together they are the stack: Reflection, where the system critiques and revises its own output before you see it. Tool Use, where it calls real functions, search, code execution, your APIs, instead of guessing. Planning, where it decomposes a task into steps and replans when one fails. Multi-Agent Collaboration, where specialized agents divide the labor, one writing, one reviewing, one testing.

Here is the part that matters for anyone holding a platform budget: these four are a maturity sequence, not a feature catalog. Reflection and Tool Use are production-reliable today. Reflection in particular is close to free, one additional critique pass in the call chain, and it is quick to implement for the gain it delivers. Planning and Multi-Agent are more powerful and meaningfully less predictable. Ng himself says he finds it hard to predict in advance what a planning agent will do. Their outputs are harder to anticipate, which means they need governance infrastructure, step-level logging, human override points, audit trails between agents, before broad deployment, not after the first incident.

Teams that treat the four patterns as a shopping list and deploy everything at once do not get four times the value. They get incidents from the unpredictable patterns, no logging to diagnose which part failed, and a stakeholder trust problem that then blocks approval for the simple, reliable patterns that would have paid for everything. Sequence matters. Each rung builds the operational muscle the next rung requires.

The corrected question

So here is the Monday-morning version. The next time an AI investment conversation opens with which model, ask instead: what workflow wraps our model calls today?

If the honest answer is nothing, single-pass prompts into a frontier model, then you are running the expensive configuration from the illustration above, and you have architecture headroom that no model upgrade will buy you. The budget conversation should move accordingly. Fund workflow design. Fund the tool registry and the function-call logging. Fund the reflection loops on production workflows. Fund the governance layer each successive pattern requires, in sequence, so that when you do reach for Planning and Multi-Agent you can operate them without generating incidents. Then, sure, upgrade the model too. The upgrade is fine. It is just not the lever.

There is one more twist worth naming, because it answers the obvious objection. Someone will point out that the models got dramatically better between 2024 and 2026, and they did. But look at how: the labs absorbed the agentic lessons into training itself. Today’s reasoning models run yesterday’s reflection and planning loops internally. The architecture dividend did not disappear; part of it moved inside the model, which is exactly why swapping among frontier peers now buys so little, and the rest of it still sits in the harness you build. Either way, the lever was never the label on the model.

And notice what this does to the competitive question from the opening. The model is a commodity; the architecture is not. The wrapper you build is specific to your workflows, your controls, your data, your value streams. It compounds, because each pattern you operationalize makes the next one cheaper to adopt. Your competitor cannot buy your architecture with a credit card. The open-weight surge everyone is currently blogging about makes the same point from the other direction: when a downloadable, permissively licensed model sits within a point of the proprietary frontier, the model layer has stopped being anyone’s moat, and the whole open-versus-closed debate is an argument about which commodity to standardize on. That asymmetry, not the model shortlist and not the license on it, is where a durable difference lives. Everyone is about to have the same build speed on paper; almost nobody differentiates there.

Where this does not apply

Two boundaries, so this reads as precise rather than absolutist.

First, sometimes the model genuinely is the constraint. Specific capability gaps on specialized tasks, hard requirements on data residency or on-premise deployment, regulatory constraints that narrow the vendor field to one or two, unit economics at extreme volume. When one of those binds, the model question is the right question. My claim is about the default case, where the models on the shortlist are near-substitutes and the debate between them is absorbing attention the architecture question deserves.

Second, and I hold this boundary firmly: architecture is the technical lever, not the whole story. A well-built agentic loop dropped into an organization that changes nothing about how it works will still disappoint, the same way every injected capability disappoints when the ways of working around it stay frozen. The returns arrive when teams re-form their work around what the loop makes possible. Architecture over model, and adaptation over both. If your organization has not done the second, the first will only get you a very sophisticated way of producing output nobody has redesigned their week around.

One question to take with you

Pull up your organization’s most recent AI investment discussion, the actual document or the actual agenda. Count the minutes or the slides spent on which model, and count the ones spent on what surrounds the model. If the first number is bigger, and in my experience it usually is, the deck is comparing the wrong variable, and the correction is worth making before the next budget cycle locks it in.
