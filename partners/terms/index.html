OpMo Advisory · Partner terms

The split, the register, the paper.

You’re here because we’ve talked, or because someone I trust sent you. Either way, this page says out loud what most networks leave to awkward phone calls: how money divides on a joint deal, and how we keep the accounting honest. Current as of August 2026. If we agree different terms on a specific deal, the deal paper wins.

The split

Finder, minder, grinder.

Three roles in every deal, and the revenue follows the roles, not the seniority of the people holding them. The frame comes from Hale and Dorr’s professional-services compensation model from the 1940s, which is still the most widely adapted answer to exactly this problem: crediting the sale separately from the delivery.

Finder 10 percent

Originated the specific opportunity: made the introduction, or surfaced the named buyer and the named problem. Published referral benchmarks run 5 to 10 percent, so this sits at the top of the standard band.

Minder 20 percent

Runs presales, closes the deal, and owns the client relationship through delivery. The minder’s corporation signs as prime and invoices, and takes no separate margin for doing so; the 20 covers the whole job, including the risk of carrying the paper.

Grinder 70 percent

Delivers the work after signature. The largest share goes here on purpose, because the delivery is the product and the fastest way to poison a network is to pay the people doing the work like subcontractors while the introducers take the cream. Published working shares run 60 to 70 percent; we chose the top.

Roles stack

A warm handoff is 10. Find it and run presales while someone else delivers is 30. Do everything yourself with no partner involvement and it’s 100, no obligation to anyone. On a $33,000 CAD engagement, a warm handoff pays the finder $3,300 CAD; finder plus minder pays $9,900 CAD. Shared finder or minder roles split equally; a shared grinder role splits pro-rata to the agreed delivery share.

Co-selling is paid, not begged

A partner who isn’t the minder and joins client-facing presales at the minder’s request earns 5 percent, taken from the minder’s 20. A finder who also shows up to the pitch earns 15. Nobody is asked to sell for free on a deal that wins.

Small favours stay free

An hour of advice, a quick review, a walk-on to one call: nobody invoices for that, and being asked is normal. When it stops being an hour, it gets raised out loud rather than carried.

All shares apply to consulting services revenue only.

Product, subscription, training-product, and sponsorship revenue stay outside this table; a services deal that bundles a partner’s product needs its own terms at signature.

The deal register

The register is the agreement.

One row per opportunity: a named buyer plus a named problem, the finder, the date, and then the minder and grinder as the deal takes shape.

What earns a claim

Registering, not knowing. Knowing a company earns nothing; a registered opportunity is everything. Register when there’s any potential for a split; an opportunity you intend to run entirely alone doesn’t need a row, though that choice carries its own risk, because if someone else registers the same buyer and problem first, they hold finder. The originating introduction defines the finder even if another partner enters the row.

Claims expire

90 days without pursuit and the claim lapses. Waiting out a posted procurement process counts as pursuit.

When a deal dies in presales

Nobody is paid, and each party bears its own presales costs, which is the standard teaming convention.

What credit covers afterward

Extensions and renewals of the original engagement, at full rate for 12 months after the first invoice, then at half rate for the next 12, then it stops. New, distinct scope spotted during delivery makes its spotter the finder of a new deal.

When two people claim the same deal

First to register wins. Both registered independently before seeing each other’s entry, and finder credit splits 50/50. Still contested, and the uninvolved partners decide by majority on a 15-minute call, logged in the register.

Above all the tie-breaks sits a plainer rule.

If a split, a role, or a workload feels wrong, you raise it with the facts rather than carrying it, and the people not involved arbitrate. The rules cover the clear cases. The fairness principle covers the rest.

Money mechanics

Collected, not billed. Paid within ten business days.

All shares are computed on collected revenue, not billed, so collection risk is shared proportionally. Shares are paid within 10 business days of each client payment. When a deal runs under an external prime’s paper, the same table applies to the net revenue reaching the group.

Everything gets papered

Every deal that carries money, whatever its size, is backed by a written teaming agreement or contract. When I prime, the sub contracts back-to-back with my corporation, mirroring the client paper on the terms that move risk: payment window, termination, notice. When you prime, I expect the same shape and I’ll sign yours if it matches.

What this page doesn’t do

Nothing here creates an exclusive arrangement, a guarantee of deal flow, or an obligation to pursue anything together. It’s the default paper we reach for so that the interesting conversation, the client’s actual problem, doesn’t get delayed by a commercial negotiation we could have settled in advance.
