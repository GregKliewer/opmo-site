You renamed the teams. You did not move the decisions.

August 14, 2026 · 6 min · Operating model

The org chart is the cheapest part of an operating model change, and it is the part everybody does first.

New names arrive. Squads, pods, tribes, streams. A product organization where there used to be a project organization. Somebody gets “Head of” in front of their title and a mandate paragraph that reads beautifully in a town hall deck.

And then, underneath, nothing moves. Funding still gets allocated once a year against a portfolio plan built the previous fall. Architecture decisions still route through a council that meets every second Tuesday. The person named as owner of the new thing can raise a flag, write a paper, and escalate. They cannot move a dollar or a person.

The failure has a name

Accountability-authority mismatch. Someone is held responsible for an outcome without control of the resources, the decisions, or the operating model required to produce it.

It’s a structural defect, not anyone’s performance problem. But few organizations clock that. Their reaction to stalled transformation is to search out and swap in a stronger leader. Guess what, they get the same result two quarters later, because the second leader is handcuffed by the same constraints as the first. Now there is real confusion. What? Is there no one out there who can lead us to success here? What gives?!

The tell is in the language… how is the role described?

Go read the mandate that came with the reorg. Read the job description that was posted for the new leadership role. Read the slide that explained the change.

Count how many times the verbs are ownership verbs, and how many times they are influence verbs. Drives alignment across teams… Partners with product to… Provides architectural oversight of… Enables the engineering organization… Sets strategy and roadmap…

None of those are bad things to do. Plenty of good roles do exactly that work. The problem starts when influence verbs are paired with accountability for an outcome that depends on delivery capacity the role does not hold. That combination is a promise nobody can keep, and everybody finds out about two quarters after the announcement, which is exactly long enough that the reorg is no longer what anyone thinks to blame.

Four questions predict the outcome, and it doesn’t take long to run through them.

Capacity. Does the accountable person control the engineering capacity that produces the outcome, or does that capacity sit in someone else’s line, carrying its own backlog and its own quarterly commitments that are set outside of this mandate?

Budget. Can they move money inside their own mandate without a trip back to a portfolio committee, and if they cannot, how long is the round trip in weeks?

Prioritization. Can they change what gets worked on next quarter, or can they only make a case that it should change and then wait to hear?

Thresholds. What is the dollar figure, or the risk classification, or the architectural scope above which they have to go ask permission? Most organizations have never written this down. If nobody can tell you the number, the honest answer is that the threshold is zero and everything is an ask.

What does not fix it

This is where I want to be blunt, because the standard responses are not just ineffective. They actively make it worse, and they are expensive.

Adding a governance layer does not fix it. A new steering committee over top of the mismatch adds a queue and a calendar to a problem that was already about queues and calendars.

Another review board does not fix it. Neither does renaming the existing one to something with “product” in it.

Expanding influence does not fix it. Giving the accountable person a seat at three more forums gives them three more meetings, not one more decision.

And adding people does not fix it. If the ownership structure is unchanged, a bigger team just distributes the same blocked decisions across more people, all of whom now also have to be coordinated. There is a real cost here that nobody books: every one of these moves obscures accountability a little further, so the next person who tries to diagnose the problem has more layers to dig through than you did.

There are really only two durable resolutions, and both are structural:

Give the accountable role direct ownership of the delivery capacity.

Narrow the mandate until it matches the control the person actually has.

The latter sounds like a retreat. And in a real sense it is. But if the organization doesn’t have the guts to place trust in its people, then it’s a more honest and effective tactic than promising transformative change without distributing the power to effect that throughout the organization.

The ironic thing that’s both funny and sad.

This is the part that makes me want to laugh and cry at the same time: we are now writing this down for the first time… for agents. We never wrote it down for people.

Every serious organization deploying AI agents this year is being told, correctly, to name the decisions embedded in the agent’s task before it runs. Which calls it can make on its own. Which ones it recommends and a named human approves. Which ones it does not touch without explicit instruction. By all means, this needs to be written down. It’s a foundational step to striking a balance between velocity and effective control, so that your newly agentic-accelerated org can truly move with both speed and safety, simultaneously. I wrote about that back in July and still believe every word I said.

Now go and ask the same question about the humans in the same organization. For the person you just named a leader in the transformation, which decisions can they make and act on without checking? Which ones do they recommend for someone else to approve? Which ones are not theirs at all?

In most places, nobody can answer. The decision rights were never named, so in practice they default to the old ones, which live with whoever held them before the reorg. The names on the boxes changed. The permissions did not.

Oh, the irony! But I guess it’s not a new one. I’ve been in the industry for over 25 years. This is not the first time that we learned faster how to handle the technology than we did how to enable the people using it. And it probably won’t be the last.

Will your transformation succeed? Here’s the quickest diagnostic to run.

If your organization is embarking on a transformational program, regardless of its nature - an acquisition that instantly brings in new tech, product model adoption, monolith to microservices architecture, and of course, AI adoption - you do not have to guess whether your organization will move decision rights along with names and titles. The record exists. Look at how you adopted agile.

Benedikt Stemmildt drew this parallel on LinkedIn earlier this summer, between the agile adoption cycle and the AI one, and I am 100% on board with it. Agile was never a technical project. The technology was a whiteboard, some sticky notes, and a two-week calendar. The hard part was organizational, and the resistance concentrated among the most experienced people, who had the most finely tuned way of working to protect. The same shape shows up every time a real operating model change comes through, whatever triggered it.

So read your own record. Did the organization pilot the change somewhere contained before rolling it out, or did it mandate it everywhere at once? Did it fund the coaching layer, or did it buy the tool licenses and declare itself transformed? Did senior leadership carry the change personally, and accept distribution of their pre-existing authority to others in the organization, or did it all get handed to a program office the month after the announcement, with explicit or tacit understanding that some executive authority is sacrosanct, and not to be questioned or touched? Was the resistance from senior practitioners engaged with, or overridden? Those people were often right that the change would disrupt how they worked, and the organizations that steamrolled them lost real institutional knowledge on the way through.

The answers will predict the success or failure of the next transformational change investment your organization makes. An organization that renamed the teams and left the decisions where they were during the agile transition will do it again. The agentic revolution will be no different.

Where this does not apply

Something genuinely new

If you are standing up a function that did not exist, there is nothing to rename and no incumbent decision rights to move. Build the ownership structure deliberately at the start, resist the urge to copy the previous model onto it, and revisit it once the team has formed something worth diagnosing.

When the names really are step one

There is a legitimate sequence where the structure gets announced first and the decision rights follow within a quarter, because you needed the shape settled before you could reassign the authority. I have seen this work. What makes it work is that the second step was scheduled, funded, and owned before the first one was announced. If the decision rights move is not on somebody’s calendar with a date on it, it is not a sequence. It is just the first step.

Ask yourself the question

Take the last reorg your organization ran. Product model adoption, a consolidation, a platform migration, an AI mandate, it genuinely does not matter which one.

What decisions moved? Not what changed on the chart. What can somebody now decide, and act on, that they could not decide before?

And if the honest answer is that nothing moved, I can guess what the result was, but we don’t need to belabor that… What I’d like you to do, in that case, is ask yourself this: if you had it to do over again, what would you go back and do differently for the people you asked to own the outcome?
