When you're not ready, you fail. Changing the trigger does not change the outcome.

August 10, 2026 · 7 min · Operating model

Adding something to an organization magnifies how well that organization works.

An organization ready to accept the addition realizes gains that compound.
An organization that is unready for the addition does not support gains at all.

In the latter case, at best, there will be no gain whatsoever. But what is more likely, really, is that you will realize real losses as the people part of the organization, suddenly experiencing frustration, overwork, and blame, burn out and shut down or quit under the pressure.

Prepared organizations accelerate, and unprepared ones don’t.

I wanted to state that as a general rule before mentioning the term “AI”. Yes, it’s true that when you add AI, specifically, to organizations these days, this effect is triggered. Prepared organizations accelerate, and unprepared ones don’t. It is, to be sure, a trigger. It’s just not the only one. Not by a long shot. But more on that later…

For now, let’s look at what everyone else is staring at these days, Artificial Intelligence. DORA found that “AI serves as a powerful amplifier in software development. It magnifies the strengths of high-performing organizations and the dysfunctions of struggling ones.” That is the headline conclusion of their ROI of AI-Assisted Software Development work, built on a decade of DORA research into real teams, doing real work, on real things. It’s clean and reliable.

Here’s what I want to say about it, though. The way that is written, it’s easy to infer that AI is the cause of this “amplifier effect”. It’s not. It’s a trigger. One among many. The root cause is really in the organizations into which AI is injected.

What the cloud platform era already taught me

I learned my lesson years earlier, during cloud platform modernization, before anyone was running the same experiment with AI. Back then, we got all excited in a similar way about the advanced, integrated security monitoring we were buying with the platform. Static analysis, dependency scanning, configuration checks, continuous monitoring. It was all there. We just had to turn it on.

And then we did, and wow. The findings! So many of them!! It was breathtaking. Almost literally. I remember a distinct shortness of breath the first time the team showed me the brand-new dashboard for the small number of products we had recently released to our young landing zone. I won’t say how many there were. But safe to say there were a lot. Way more than we thought there would be. I looked across the table at the single security engineer on the team and he looked back, dumbstruck. There were no words.

The sheer volume of work to remediate everything was a lot to do, clearly, and our engineers were overworked getting it done. But that’s not the real story. The real story is the stress that that put on our most senior security people. AND that it didn’t abate after the initial queue of findings was chewed through. The new tools did their jobs. Findings were found faster. But that did not automatically equate to remediations done.

The real story is the stress that that put on our most senior security people.

The tools weren't the problem. The problem was that every finding, however trivial, arrived as a decision for someone to make. Interpret it, triage it, negotiate it, document why it was tolerated. This all remained real work for real people. Improved detection was increasing decision demand exponentially. But nothing was being done to relieve the burden on the people involved.

Nothing was being done to relieve the burden on the people involved.

And that’s not all. Our security experts were inundated with both difficult AND trivial decisions simultaneously. And the volume split of that was weighted quite substantially to the trivial. We were buying visibility and paying for it in expert attention, occupying our best minds with more and more “work” reviewing small, repetitive things over and over again. Expert attention was the scarcest thing we had in that whole dang situation, and we were spending it on the trivial. We took steps to improve our security posture and were, in actuality, reducing it by degrading the organization’s capacity to detect and address novel threats, keeping our security experts’ eyes constantly fixed on a giant and growing backlog of alerts and buzzers and alarms occupying their attention.

In the end, the fix was not in the tooling. Or any tooling at all. It was in changing how things were done in the organization. Rather than sticking with our pre-cloud way of relying on the product line teams deploying products to the platform to remediate findings, we changed our practices to have the platform team embed remediations in the platform so that a finding found once could never be found again. That’s ultimately how we drove the number of findings down, alleviated the pressure on our senior security people, and refocused their attention on protecting us against novel threats. It wasn’t anything we did with the tooling. It was changes to the organization we injected the tooling into that did the trick.

Rereading the DORA numbers with this in mind

DORA, citing Stanford research, found a 35-40% productivity gain on simple greenfield tasks, but 10% or less on complex legacy code. Read as a claim about AI, that sounds like a capability ceiling.

But I think it could say a lot more about the organizations doing the work. Greenfield means clean context and, most importantly, the distinct likelihood that a new team was formed to do the job. Legacy, on the other hand, almost certainly means context that no longer matches the capabilities of the AI tooling injected into it and boundaries set before work started moving through the pipeline in entirely new ways. And THAT explains the different results from the teams working on greenfield vs legacy applications. It’s the same AI injected in both cases. In legacy cases, there are structural impediments to the work moving through the organization that the new capability finds, piling up work there. Teams operating in greenfield simply form their ways of working around the capability in the first place.

Where the rule doesn’t apply

New teams

As we said, a genuinely new team should be able to self-organize around the capabilities it’s given. So buy the thing, move fast, and revisit the team once it has formed a structure worth diagnosing. It should go without saying not to CTRL-C + CTRL-V a previous operating model onto the new team and expect it to work (as we did during cloud adoption… learn from the mistakes of others).

Grace period

There’s also a legitimate window before you hit your first bottleneck in existing ways of working that you can take advantage of to get going faster. This buys you a short period of time to redesign your practices before they become a problem. But be warned, time is short, and the runway will vary widely depending on the new thing you’re injecting into the team.

The trigger is the variable

Last week I wrote about the gap between what an organization funds and what it can absorb. This article is about a layer underneath that. It isn’t that an ill-designed operating model fails to absorb new capabilities you inject into it. It’s that it actively magnifies negative results.

And here’s the main point, where we started above: the trigger matters far less than we give it credit for. Yes, AI comes with huge potential to revolutionize our work. But cloud migration has high potential too. As does a product model transition. A consolidation pulling four capabilities into one function. An acquisition. And so on. And so on. And so on. Different trigger, same results, because the amplification is a property of the organization in place on arrival, not of the new thing we drop on it.

Failure is avoidable

I shouldn’t have to say it, but I will. The low returns on complex and legacy applications are not a ceiling. You don’t have to treat them as one. It’s distinctly possible to beat those numbers if you invest enough in operating model realignment along with what you invest in licensing for new tooling.

So: what's arriving at your organization this quarter, and what can we predict your organization, as it exists today, will do with that investment?
