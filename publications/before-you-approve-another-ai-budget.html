Before you approve another AI budget, ask this.

July 21, 2026 · 6 min · AI budgets

Chances are your organization is currently planning to adopt agentic AI. Likely, someone has tallied up the expected cost. Whether you're at a startup comfortable with a simple spreadsheet tally (or something scrawled on the back of a napkin; I have been there!), or a stodgy old enterprise that made you fill out a huge, templated spreadsheet from Finance and a set of standard slides for the Executive Committee before budget was approved, one pattern keeps showing up in how those budgets get built: something in the neighbourhood of 70% of the spend on technology, and about 30% on everything else needed to make that technology actually work inside your organization.

I want to be precise about what that number is and isn't. It's not a verified statistic. It's Joao L.'s read of the pattern, shared on LinkedIn this June, and it's not in any survey I can point you to. But it matches what I've watched happen up close, more than once, so I'm passing it along as exactly that: a practitioner's observation, not a finding. I don't know if 70/30 is the precise right split, and I'd be suspicious of anyone who claims to know it to the percentage point. What I do believe, from watching one of these cycles play out before: by the time an organization is 1-2 years into living with a genuinely new technology, the true cost of the change it demands will exceed the cost of the technology itself. That's my bet, not a fact, and I'll tell you what would change my mind at the end.

Here's what's likely counted in the technology majority: models and inference spend, tokens or self-hosting hardware, agent orchestration and workflow tooling, data infrastructure for retrieval and context-keeping, engineering work, and new observability, security, testing, and FinOps tooling too. Here's what's usually counted on the other side: training. That's often where the accounting stops. Training alone. Here's what's rarely tallied but always shows up eventually anyway: FinOps isn't just a tool, it's the governance work of deciding what gets AI spend, when, and what stays manual to hold the line on budget. Team and role redesign. The productivity dip while teams re-form, re-storm, re-norm, and emerge once again into high performance around a new way of working. Decision latency while nobody's quite sure yet who owns which call. None of that shows up in a tooling line item, and all of it is real spend, just deferred and unbudgeted.

The "strategy" most enterprises are establishing for their AI programs is clear - and I wish I could make those quotes bigger and more ugly – they will acquire for their people new technology that can revolutionize how they deliver products to the market, train them on it, and then the employees will figure out how to get it done within all of the existing processes, practices, and constraints in the organization, all by themselves, without any further support. Deloitte's State of AI in the Enterprise 2026 backs up how common this is, at scale: 84% of companies have not redesigned jobs around AI capabilities, and among companies making any adjustment to their talent strategy at all, 53% are simply focusing on educating employees to raise AI fluency rather than touching how work actually gets done.

This is a pattern I have seen play out across multiple "revolutionary" technology waves that, in the end, realized far less value than originally promised for most who invested in them. Take the Web API craze that swept across pretty much all industry verticals in the 2010s. Emulating the success of big tech selling direct access to online services that companies worldwide incorporated into their own product offerings with minimal support, enterprises across Finance, Engineering, Travel and Transportation, Logistics, Manufacturing - virtually all private and public sectors - bought and rolled out API portals built to make access to their data and systems as easily accessible to software engineers as possible. I was at the tip of the spear in that revolution, selling standardized technology suites tailored to facilitate all the technology things needed to make the dream happen: automatic discovery and documentation, standardized publication, access controls for both humans and machines, monetization integrations, tailored observability, and on and on. The technology was powerful and easy to use. It was all there for my customers to get the job done. Or so I thought. Then I joined organizations that had bought the technology, and what I saw shocked me. Mostly empty portals. And of what was available on them, sadly little utilization. What I discovered was that the tech was fine. It was deployed and working as promised. The problem was that the organizations themselves were constrained from adopting the technology and using it within the contexts they were deployed. In highly regulated financial services where I worked, one of the top constraints was regulatory compliance. For most of what would have been valuable to provide to a broader community of developers, the costs to comply with regulations proved so considerable, and the potential returns so un-guarantee-able, that business cases to go to production never got approved. Everything stopped at the prototype level. Almost always. And what did make it to production was, yes, completely safe, but also of so little value that it just sat there, gathering dust.

Sound familiar? The same pattern is playing out now, a decade later, with agentic AI. A rush to invest in the technology with no consideration, much less any plan for investment, in tailoring how it is used for the specific business contexts and organizational processes and practices on which it lands. The same Deloitte study cited above tells us that only 21% of companies report a mature governance model for agentic AI, even though 74% expect to be using agentic AI at least moderately within two years. Left unaddressed, that combination tends to produce the same pattern I watched with Web APIs: an enthusiastic push to prototyping, followed by stalling out before production. In my mind, that is guaranteed to happen again, unless we learn from the past. Returning to the Web API example, we learned, very late, and the hard way, that there was a lot of un-scoped work needed to ensure regulatory compliance for including more valuable data and services in APIs. It wasn't that our regulators prohibited the inclusion of sensitive data in Web API interfaces, or doing relatively risky operations through them, like moving money or binding agreements. We just needed to be able to demonstrate to them that we had strong and sufficient control over what we were doing. So, what did we need to do? You got it. We went back to the well to get fresh buy in, and new funding, to build those controls, prove their efficacy, and enable our governance structure to validate all of that along the way. These are not small things to do. And so it took years to "unstick" stalled programs. My advice to organizations writing AI-generated benefits into their forecasts this year is to avoid making the same mistake we made in the Web API revolution. For those return forecasts to come in on time, you need to write in significant effort to overhaul your governance practices in year 1, otherwise those returns will not materialize for years to come.

One boundary on all of this: a favorable budget split doesn't guarantee success, and an unfavorable one isn't a verdict on its own. It's a strong enough warning to justify a closer look before you commit, not a reason to kill the business case outright.

For now, I propose a simple test for any enterprise that is medium-sized or bigger: check your agentic AI business case. Does technology tooling spend outweigh organization, process, and people enablement spend? Hit pause. Look again. Yes, increasing forecasted spend on org redesign will mute returns somewhat. But in my book, high confidence of getting returns on the books quickly is much better than rolling the dice that they will materialize at all.

What would change my mind: if organizations that go in accounting for all of this upfront, FinOps governance, role redesign, the re-forming dip, decision latency, and the rest, still find that tooling costs dominate over a 1-2 year horizon, then I'm wrong, and the ratio itself isn't the signal worth watching.
