The alignment diagnostic
Why an outsider

There are two reasons to bring in an outsider, and they get confused constantly.

Legitimacy

Board-level cover for a recommendation your team already formed. Large advisory firms are built to deliver this, and they deliver it well.

Absorption

Someone to hold the unpopular position, be the named source of the uncomfortable move, and leave with the resentment attached to them instead of to the people who stay.

If you were hired as the change mandate, you don’t need the first. You are the legitimacy. So you turn the firm down, and you’re right to. But then it’s tempting to conclude that you don’t need outside help at all. That’s the part that can cost you.

01

The loss lands.

Transformation imposes loss. Moved reporting lines, removed discretion, changed roles, exits. Every one of those decisions has a loser who knows exactly who made it. And everyone who remains is needed to deliver in the new ways of working. Every ounce of resentment they carry forward fuels resistance to changes essential for success.

02

The trades begin.

For those who lead the transformation in front of operating the thing themselves, tradeoffs start around month nine. A restructure softened. A role change deferred. A conversation not had. None of it gets written down as a decision to slow the transformation, but the effects are unavoidable.

03

The swap changes nothing.

Two to four quarters later the program has under-delivered, the organization decides it has a talent problem, and swaps the leader. The replacement inherits the same relational economics and lands in the same place on the same timeline. This is when stalling turns into regression and failure.

And everyone is surprised and disappointed that the second leader fails just like the first.

I have been both of those positions, making those same trades. Not as a failure of nerve - I’d make some of them again, because the relationships were real and I needed them to ship anything at all - but due entirely to the position I was placed in. A no-win situation. Which is the point. The incentive is structural. It does not care how good the leader is, and it did not care how good I was.

Two days a week

A standing seat in your governance forum, embedded with the leadership team, owning a named workstream end to end. The calls inside that workstream come from me and land with my name on them. Accountable for the workstream, not for the operating model around it.

Transformation Lead

Outcome accountability

2.5 days a week

The embedded seat plus accountability for the operating-model outcome, and for training the person who inherits it. Every uncomfortable call across the model is mine to make and mine to carry out of the building when I go. You keep accountability for the destination. What moves outside is the diagnosis stated plainly, the naming of what has to stop, and the pressure that lets a hard call arrive as an external finding rather than as one colleague choosing against another.

The seat exists to be vacated.

Every mandate names the condition that ends it, in writing, before the work starts. What the operator has to be able to do without me. What has to be running. What evidence counts.

You judge when it’s been met. I don’t.

I don’t get to be the one who decides I’m still needed.

The term runs as long as the transformation does, and the big ones run for many quarters into years. The exit test doesn’t cap that and isn’t meant to. What it fixes is narrower: nobody in the room is paid to say the work isn’t finished yet.

The fit test

This works when the changes take something away from people whose cooperation you still need after the change is in place. A platform org centralizing and converging on technology and standards. A product-model transition that has stalled. An AI investment promising big returns in the form of speed and efficiency.

If you don’t have someone to operate under the new model, then by all means, hire.

But don’t make the mistake so many in your position do and saddle that new hire with the weight of hard feelings they need not bear in the new operating model. Let me take much of that away with me after the job is done, and the new operator is comfortable in their seat, so that the object of resistance leaves cleanly, and you inherit an organization amenable to the work in front of it.

Track record

Three technology waves in Canadian financial services.

AI-First Microservices

TD & CIBC

2016–2019

Set enterprise API strategy and governance through the API-native wave: bank-wide API standards, an early external developer platform, and modernization of core services into microservices patterns.

How, and how it was left

CIBC's core modernization program was in stasis on arrival: churning on technical decisions between multiple options, each with its own stakeholder advocates. I clarified platform boundaries and decision rights, and held each owner accountable for unblocking their own platform decisions and moving forward on a roadmap for change.

The proof it landed: program was unstuck, and projects moved through implementation to completion. Workload migrated to the API platform over time, and legacy XML appliance infrastructure serving SOAP services was decommissioned.

How it was left: decisions cleared at the platform level, owners accountable for their own paths, and roadmap for change in place.

Product Model

Interac

2021–2026

Built Interac's first enterprise-architecture practice, then led a 50+ person Platform-as-Product organization through a full transformation to a product operating model, in a mission-critical, regulated payments environment.

How, and how it was left

I scaled the company-wide project-to-product transformation into Technology & Operations by establishing a Platform-as-Product delivery model: the platform team realigned to operate as a product team serving the internal product line teams as customers, with roles, cadences, and ways of working redefined to deliver in the new operating model.

The proof it landed: internal customer NPS was minus 25 after the platform organization's first quarter, aggregated across three squads. Two quarters later it was plus 15. All three squads improved, and two finished in positive territory rated Good and Excellent on the internal survey.

How it was left: a 50+ person platform organization running as a product organization, delivering more valuable platform features, faster, driving down product lead times, cycle times, and time-to-findings remediation across all supported product lines.

AI-Native

Independent practice

now

AI-Native Transformation practice serving Canadian financial services. Doctrine and diagnostics for operating models designed to absorb AI.

How, and how it was left

After establishing the Platform Delivery and Engineering organization at Interac, I turned to the new challenge of incorporating cutting edge technology to optimize team delivery. We incorporated AI into the engineering SDLC, focussing on compressing discovery cycles, reinforcing weaknesses with agentic support, and tailoring client self-service with prompt-response interfaces.

The proof it landed: discovery cycles were reduced by more than 50%, tailored skills for improving test coverage and verifying controls were positively received by engineers, and client feedback on pilot prompt-response interfaces was overwhelmingly positive.

How it was left: AI accelerated SDLC emerging from pilot and ready for scaling to the engineering organization.
