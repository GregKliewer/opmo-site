The middle of the dumbbell is just the handle

September 8, 2026 · 6 min · Agentic delivery

Last week I argued that discovery is the heaviest plate on the dumbbell. This week I want to finish the shape, because the shape is the whole argument, and I’m putting it on stage in Toronto on Wednesday. So let me get it on the record first, in writing, where you can hold me to it.

Here’s the shape in one sentence. The build phase of the software lifecycle got dramatically faster and, in the same stroke, stopped being something you can win on. The two ends didn’t move. So the weight is at the ends, and the middle is just the handle.

Every dumbbell in the gym has the same handle

I want to run with that image a bit. Nobody has ever walked into a gym and picked a dumbbell for its handle. The handle is the part you’re not supposed to notice. It’s the same knurled steel on every rack in every gym in the world. I say this with some discomfort, because I spent the better part of twenty years worried about the metaphorical handle of the SDLC. Platform, pipelines, delivery tooling, developer experience. Good work, work I’m proud of, and for most of that time there were real gains to be made there, because the build was the constraint. It’s not anymore. Thanks, Anthropic!

Every serious source I’ve read on agentic delivery this year, vendor and practitioner alike, agrees on one thing: the constraint has moved from writing the code to specifying it precisely up front and verifying it afterward. Anthropic’s own playbook opens with “Code is no longer the bottleneck.” And your competitors bought the same coding agents you did, in the same quarter, at the same price. I made that point about the models a couple weeks ago, and it is just as true one level up. If everyone’s build got faster, nobody’s build got faster relative to anyone else. Speed in the middle is the price of admission now. It’s not a way to differentiate.

Which is why adding agents to an otherwise unchanged process gets you a bump and then a ceiling. The sources agree on that too, and it matches what I’ve seen every time a team points a new tool at an old workflow. The gain is real, and then it stops, and everyone is a little confused about why the quarterly numbers didn’t skyrocket like the demos seemed to promise.

Why I load the right end with approval

Rich Mironov, whose barbell piece I credited last week, loads his right end with go-to-market. In his world, that’s right. If you’re shipping a product into a market, the question after the build is whether anyone will buy it, and the product job gets heavier there.

My world is regulated financial services, and the question after the build in that world is different. It’s whether you’re allowed to have the thing at all. Security review, compliance, risk, change management, the audit trail that has to exist before anything touches production. That’s the right end I draw, and I draw it there for two reasons.

The first is that it’s where the pile-up is. I’ve written about this twice already, so I’ll be brief. Give a team a build engine that runs many times faster than it did last year, and an approval pipeline that runs at exactly the speed it ran last year, and the output queues up in front of the people accountable for control. They either fall behind or they start rubber-stamping. Those are the two options they’re left with. Nobody chose that for them. The people who funded the engine just never funded the end it feeds into.

The second reason is the one I actually want to spend this post on. The approval end is tractable today, and discovery isn’t. Discovery is a judgment problem. You can accelerate real customer contact, but the deciding is still a human deciding, and last week’s post was mostly about protecting that. Approval is different. A large share of what happens at the approval end is mechanically checkable. Did the change touch a data classification it isn’t allowed to touch? Does the test evidence exist? Is the provenance recorded? Does the configuration match the policy? Those are questions a pipeline can answer.

I’ve watched exactly that move happen once already, with security controls, in a platform organization I was part of. The controls started as a review board and a queue. They ended as modules in the pipeline that ran on every change whether anyone remembered to ask or not. Compliance through implementation is what I’ve taken to calling it. It worked because it made the compliant path the easiest path through a developer’s day, and it didn’t work until it did.

That move hasn’t been made for the AI-era approval end in most shops I talk to. Not because it’s hard. Because it isn’t what vendors sell, and it isn’t what a board can see on a slide. The engine got funded. The end it feeds into didn’t.

What makes the right end heavy

Two things, and neither of them is the tools being bad.

The first is volume. Agents, left alone, write more code than the problem needs. They’re built to solve the problem, not to minimize the artifact that solves it. So the reviewer at the right end isn’t just reviewing more changes. Each change is longer than a human would have written, which means more attack surface, more to audit, and more to explain to a regulator when something goes wrong. One practitioner I follow has published an agent skill that forces three questions before any code gets written (is code even needed, does something built-in already do this, what’s the minimal version) and reports large reductions in output with no loss in quality. I haven’t verified his numbers, so I won’t quote them. But the direction is right. Code volume is a governance variable, and almost nobody is measuring it.

The second is that verification effort has to be graded, and most shops either ban or bless instead. The loud debate right now is whether agents should write their own tests. One camp says never. The other says sure, with review. Both are one rule for every situation, and that’s the problem with both. Grade by the decision the code is making instead. Reversible, low-stakes work: let the agent write its tests, and have a human review the code and the tests together. Regulated, high-stakes, hard-to-reverse work: an independent test suite the agent had no hand in. Grade it, don’t ban it.

Where the first movers are

Here’s the claim I’m putting on stage on Wednesday, so I’ll state it here first. Differentiation in 2027 won’t come from building faster in the middle. It will come from occupying the two ends while they’re still mostly empty. And of the two ends, the approval end is the one you can occupy this quarter, because the precedent exists and the checks are mostly mechanical. Discovery is harder and less solved, and that’s exactly why it stays open longer.

The quickest way to find out which end is yours: take one value stream with agent output in production, and put two numbers next to each other. How long the build takes now versus a year ago, and how long an idea takes to reach production now versus a year ago. If the first number fell and the second didn’t - guess what?! - you’ve been paying for the handle of your dumbbell, dumbbell! And without that weight on the end, you’ve been getting no stronger than before.

Where this doesn’t apply

If your build hasn’t sped up yet, none of this has arrived for you. Your constraint is still in the middle, and you should fix it there. If you don’t have the handle, there’s no dumbbell at all :)

If you’re shipping software into a market where the thing that decides your fate is a customer’s wallet, Rich’s right end is your right end, and go-to-market deserves the weight. I’m not arguing with that. I’m describing a different room, with a table, and almost everyone around that table is about making sure nothing goes wrong with what you release and give little thought to whether anyone buys it.

And if the stakes are low enough that approval really is thin, one reviewer, one owner, nothing regulated, then the right end is light for you and should stay light. Adding gates you don’t need is how governance theater starts.

The bet

My bet: by the end of 2027, the shops that are visibly ahead will be the ones whose approval end runs at something close to agent speed, and you’ll be able to tell from outside, because their idea-to-production time will have fallen and not just their build time. What would change my mind is a shop that got there by buying more speed in the middle. More agents, better models, faster pipelines, same approval process, and elapsed time fell anyway. If that shop exists, the handle matters more than I think it does, and I’ll say so.

Wednesday in Toronto

On Wednesday, Csaba Bereczki, Jamie Beach and I put the whole dumbbell on stage at FOST Toronto, co-located with apidays. Both ends, a fictional company, four interventions, and one paid feature that gets erased from existence on day 46 because the number written down on day zero said so. If you’re there, come find us.

Two questions to close on. Think about the last change that waited in your approval queue longer than it took to build. Not the exceptional one, the ordinary one. How long did it wait, and what was it actually waiting for? And then the harder one. If you could have moved one week of the money you spent on the engine to the end the engine feeds into, what would you have bought with it? I have my answer. It’s a boring answer, and that’s sort of the point. Nobody claps for a pipeline module at a demo. But it’s the plate, not the handle.
