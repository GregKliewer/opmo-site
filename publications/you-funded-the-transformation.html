You funded the transformation. Nobody checked whether your operating model could absorb it.

August 4, 2026 · 6 min · Operating model

A LOT of effort in change programs goes into choosing what to buy. Far too little goes into checking whether the organization receiving it can take it on. That's backwards. And IMHO it's the most common failure mode that prevents companies from realizing the returns promised by big investments.

The fixation makes sense. I have been there myself, multiple times, and perseverated the same way myself. Picking the model, the platform, the vendor, the program, that's the decision that feels like the decision. It has options you can compare, a price, a business case, and a date. It's legible to a steering committee in a way that "our review forum meets every two weeks and that's now the constraint" is not. And the choice does matter. Buying the wrong thing is a real failure mode. No argument there.

But we also need to consider what happens after the tooling choice is made. The State of AI in Product 2026 survey, run by Product Circle and Product Institute across 309 product professionals in April and May of this year, found that 87.7% of organizations have adopted AI tools. Only 36.1% say it has strengthened their operating model. That's a 50-point gap between buying the thing and getting anything structural out of it. The sample skews toward AI-aware leaders and isn't population weighted, which is roughly what you'd expect from a survey about AI answered by people who opted into a survey about AI. Treat it as directional. But directional at a 50 point spread... where there's smoke you just gotta think there's fire too.

Only 19% of the same group described their operating model as mature and healthy before any of this arrived. So roughly four in five organizations funded an acceleration into a structure they already knew was uneven.

I've watched this from the inside more than once, and it goes the same way every time. Every. Single. Time. Funding clears. Tooling lands. But no one has done anything with the teams expected to absorb them. They're the same size they were last quarter, carrying the same backlog, reporting into the same review forum that meets every two weeks, waiting on the same one person who has to approve anything that touches production. Nothing about the structure or ways of working changed. The only thing that changed is how much is now arriving at the reviewer...

Oh, sorry, one more thing changes too: now everyone is surprised and disappointed that nothing is shipping faster than before.

A structure that was merely uneven at the old pace becomes the binding constraint when work arrives faster. The queue was always there. Nobody noticed, because nothing was backing up in it. And now - surprise, surprise! - there is no budget or people assigned to changing the operating model to harness the tool given to the team.

So the big question to ask before you approve the next big investment promising big, big returns isn't whether it's the right thing to invest in. That's the easy question. And the answer is usually pretty easy to arrive at.

What I want you to do is ask the question that few of us have asked in the past: what has to be true structurally for that investment to land with the desired results.

Decision rights. Who can approve this without asking anyone else, and how long does that take today? If the answer involves a forum that meets monthly, you've capped the return before you've started.

Capacity. Which team absorbs the new work, and what came off their plate to make room? "They'll absorb it" is not a capacity model. It's a hope.

Cadence. How often does anyone check whether this is working, against outcomes rather than delivery status? If your review cycle is slower than the thing you just funded, you'll find out late and expensively.

Accountability. The person named as owner, can they move budget and people, or can they only escalate? Ownership without authority is a slower version of no owner at all.

Metrics. Are you measuring what got shipped, or what changed? The survey has a tell on this one. Among organizations that called their operating model mature and healthy, 54.8% track time to market as something that improved, against 21.4% of the immature or ad hoc group. That's a correlation, not a cause. But it tells you what "mature and healthy" actually stands in for: the habit of adjusting how work gets done, and checking whether the adjustment took. The healthy organizations aren't doing better because of the structure they started with. They're doing better because they can see, and change, what they're doing.

None of this is an argument against buying, by the way. If the constraint really is capability, if you're standing up something new with a team built for it, then the choice is the decision and the structure question is thin. The fixation is also rational in the first 90 days of anything. You have to pick something before you can find out what it lands on, and I'd rather work with someone who bought the less-than-perfect tool decisively than someone still comparing vendors in month nine. What's just not excusable, in my mind, is stopping at the tooling budget. The organizational redesign that lets your people harness the new tool needs its own sequence and its own budget, in money and in people.

What would change my mind: evidence that organizations with weak structures which adopted heavily then improved those structures because of the adoption. If the thing you fund fixes the operating model instead of exposing it, sequencing matters much less than I think it does, and the check I'm describing is just extra work. Again, I've never seen it go that way. But then again, it's pretty rare that I get called in as part of the initial planning and selection phase. It happens. And I love it when it does. But more often I get called in after it has gone the other way. So my sample space is not necessarily that neutral. I get that.

One thing about the numbers here. They all come from AI adoption. I want to make it very clear: this isn't a problem specific to AI. It's just that AI is what's being surveyed right now. Swap the trigger and nothing changes. A cloud migration that hits every infrastructure milestone and misses every business case behind it. A product model transition where the teams got renamed and the funding calendar didn't move. A consolidation that pulls four capabilities into one function and inherits four decision paths. Same gap, different trigger.

Swap "AI rollout" for "cloud migration", "product model transition", or "consolidation" at the beginning of the "So you invested in _________ ..." sentence, and without investment in operating model alignment, that sentence will always end in "...and you were disappointed in the results."

So here is my advice. Take whatever you funded most recently. For many of you it might be agentic AI. For others, product model adoption. Still others, cloud migration. Maybe a major SaaS investment. It hardly matters...

Now ask yourself: what operating model was in place then? What team or teams and systems were supposed to absorb it?

And then, critically, what did you set aside, both in terms of time and effort, to adjust the operating model and ways of working once the new investment was made? If the honest answer is nothing, or very little (like training alone for the people expected to pick up and use the new thing) you already have your finding. And quite likely, the root of any disappointment you felt in the returns on that investment.

That's the first step to avoiding the same pattern on your next big investment.
